LeanDojo

Theorem Proving with Retrieval-Augmented Language Models — Yang, Swope, Gu, Chalamala, Song, Yu, Godil, Prenger, Anandkumar. NeurIPS 2023 (Datasets & Benchmarks Track, Oral).

Best-First Search Retrieval-Augmented Lean / mathlib NeurIPS 2023
← Back to Lab 02: Research Connections

TL;DR

Search, applied to mathematics instead of mazes

LeanDojo is two things: a toolkit that turns Lean's mathlib library into 98,734 machine-readable theorems and proofs, and ReProver — a language model that proves new theorems by proposing one proof step at a time and searching over the results with best-first search, the same family of algorithm as Greedy in Lab 02.

The problem it targets: prior LLM theorem provers were mostly closed (private code, data, compute) and bad at picking the right lemma out of a huge math library. LeanDojo open-sources the whole pipeline and adds retrieval to fix that second problem.

Same three ingredients

From grids to proofs

Every search algorithm in Lab 01 & 02 needs the same three things: a node, an action, and something ranking the frontier. LeanDojo swaps out what fills each slot — the algorithm doesn't change.

Search conceptGrid / graph search (Lab 01–02)LeanDojo / ReProver
Search nodeA state — a square, a puzzle configurationA proof state: the current goal(s) + hypotheses in Lean, e.g. n : ℕ ⊢ gcd n n = n
ActionA move — go left, slide a tileA tactic: a Lean command that transforms the goal, e.g. simp [gcd] or rw [gcd_comm]
Who proposes actionsThe successor function (fixed, mechanical)A language model, generating several candidate tactics per state
Frontier priorityh(n), or f(n)=g(n)+h(n)The model's cumulative confidence (log-probability) in the tactic sequence so far
What narrows the branching factor—Premise retrieval: instead of guessing a lemma name from scratch, retrieve likely-relevant ones from mathlib first
Goal testReached the goal stateNo open goals remain — the proof is complete

This destroys a common misconception: search isn't just for route planning. Any problem you can break into states + actions + a way to rank candidates can be searched this way.

Why retrieval matters

Narrowing the branching factor

mathlib has 130,262 premises (definitions, lemmas, theorems) available to cite. Most aren't relevant, or aren't even in scope, for a given goal — on average, 33,160 are "accessible" per theorem. Asking a language model to guess the right one from scratch is like Greedy search with an enormous branching factor b. ReProver's retriever narrows that down to the top 100 likely-relevant premises before the tactic generator even runs.

Does it work?

Pass@1 on the LeanDojo Benchmark

Two test splits: random (ordinary held-out theorems) and novel_premises (theorems needing at least one premise never seen in that combination during training — the harder generalization test).

Retrieval's contribution is the gap between the two ReProver bars: +3.6pp on the easy split, +3.1pp on the hard one — small in absolute terms, but note how much further ahead ReProver already is of GPT-4 used zero-shot, especially on novel_premises (26.3% vs 7.4%). GPT-4 has broad math knowledge but no way to search Lean's specific proof state space; ReProver does.

Why this belongs in Lab 02

Nothing about the search algorithm changed to make this work on mathematics instead of a grid. What changed is what fills in state, action, and frontier priority — and, because the branching factor here is enormous (thousands of possible lemmas per step), retrieval had to be added just to make search tractable at all. That's the same lesson as choosing a good heuristic in Lab 02: a better priority function means less wasted search.
